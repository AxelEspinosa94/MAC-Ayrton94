# 1.1 Industrial Revolution: Analytical Approach  
### **Principle — Analytical Paradigm**
The Industrial Revolution introduced an analytical, reductionist approach to problem‑solving:
- Systems are decomposed into independent parts.
- Optimization focuses on improving individual components.
- Efficiency, mechanization, and standardization dominate decision-making.

### **Doctrine — “Machine Era”**
Key doctrines:
1. **Mechanistic worldview:** organizations behave like machines.  
2. **Determinism:** outcomes are predictable if components are understood.  
3. **Linear causality:** small changes produce proportional effects.  
4. **Specialization:** tasks divided into isolated, optimized units.

### **Theorem — Analytical Decomposition**
If a system can be decomposed into independent subsystems, then:

$$
\text{Global performance} = \sum \text{Local performances}
$$

This principle underlies classical optimization and early operations research.

---

# 1.2 Postindustrial Revolution: Systems Approach  
### **Principle — Systemic Paradigm**
The postindustrial era introduced a holistic, integrative approach:
- Systems are networks of interdependent components.
- Optimization requires understanding interactions and feedback loops.
- Emphasis on adaptability, information flow, and complexity.

### **Doctrine — “Systems Era”**
Key doctrines:
1. **Holism:** the whole is more than the sum of its parts.  
2. **Interdependence:** components influence each other dynamically.  
3. **Feedback:** systems self-regulate through information loops.  
4. **Emergence:** new behaviors arise from interactions, not components.

### **Theorem — Systemic Interaction**
If components interact nonlinearly:

$$
\text{Global performance} \neq \sum \text{Local performances}
$$

Thus, optimization must consider the entire system.

# 1.3 Concept of a System  
### **Definition — System**
A **system** is a set of interrelated components working together toward a common objective.

### **Properties of Systems**
1. **Boundary:** separates system from environment.  
2. **Inputs:** resources entering the system.  
3. **Outputs:** results produced by the system.  
4. **Structure:** arrangement of components.  
5. **Behavior:** dynamic evolution over time.  
6. **Feedback:** information used to adjust performance.

### **Theorem — System Identity**
A system is defined by:

$$
\text{System} = (\text{Components},\ \text{Relationships},\ \text{Purpose})
$$

### **Examples**
- Biological systems  
- Economic systems  
- Manufacturing systems  
- Transportation networks  
- Information systems  

---

# 1.4 System Elements: Objectives, Environment, Resources, Components

### **Definition — Objective**
The desired outcome or purpose of the system.

### **Definition — Environment**
External factors influencing the system but not controlled by it.

### **Definition — Resources**
Inputs required for system operation (time, money, materials, information).

### **Definition — Components**
Subsystems or elements performing specific functions.

### **Theorem — System Functionality**
A system operates effectively when:

$$
\text{Resources} \xrightarrow{\text{Components}} \text{Outputs aligned with Objectives}
$$

---

# 1.5 Systems Approach: Basic Concepts and Premises

### **Principle — Systems Thinking**
Systems thinking emphasizes:
- Interconnections  
- Feedback loops  
- Nonlinearity  
- Dynamic behavior  
- Global optimization  

### **Premises**
1. **Totality:** decisions must consider the entire system.  
2. **Synergy:** interactions can amplify or reduce effects.  
3. **Hierarchy:** systems contain subsystems and belong to supersystems.  
4. **Equifinality:** multiple paths can lead to the same outcome.  
5. **Open systems:** systems exchange energy, matter, and information with the environment.

### **Theorem — Global Optimization Premise**
Optimizing subsystems independently does not guarantee optimal system performance.

# 1.6 System Design and Improvement

### **Definition — System Design**
Process of structuring components, resources, and interactions to achieve objectives.

### **Steps for System Design**
1. Problem definition  
2. System boundary identification  
3. Component specification  
4. Resource allocation  
5. Model construction  
6. Evaluation and simulation  
7. Implementation  
8. Monitoring and feedback

### **Definition — System Improvement**
Modifying structure or processes to enhance performance.

### **Theorem — Iterative Improvement**
System improvement is iterative:

$$
\text{Design} \rightarrow \text{Implement} \rightarrow \text{Evaluate} \rightarrow \text{Redesign}
$$

# 1.7 Origin, Historical Development, and Meaning of Operations Research (OR)

### **Definition — Operations Research**
A discipline applying mathematical, statistical, and computational methods to optimize decision-making in complex systems.

### **Historical Origins**
- Born during WWII for military logistics, resource allocation, and strategy.  
- Later expanded to industry, economics, transportation, health, and engineering.

### **Theorem — OR Problem-Solving Process**
Operations Research follows a structured methodology:

1. **Problem formulation**  
2. **Model construction**  
3. **Solution via optimization or simulation**  
4. **Validation**  
5. **Implementation**  
6. **Monitoring and feedback**

### **Principle — OR as Applied Systems Theory**
Operations Research is the practical application of systems thinking to decision-making.
